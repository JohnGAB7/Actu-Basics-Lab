Why does average claim cost become more predictable with 10,000 policies?

Insuring one phone is a gamble: the insurer pays €0 or €500. With 10,000 phones, average cost becomes much more predictable. Why?

Learning objectives

  • Describe policy cost as a random variable.
  • Calculate its expected value and standard deviation.
  • Explain how uncertainty in average cost decreases as portfolio size grows.
  • Identify the conditions and limitations of risk pooling.

1. One policy: an uncertain outcome

Each phone has a 2% annual probability of theft. Each theft costs exactly €500. Claim cost for one policy is therefore €0 with probability 98%, or €500 with probability 2%.

E[X] = 0.98 × 0 + 0.02 × 500 = €10
Var(X) = E[X²] − E[X]² = 0.02 × 500² − 10² = 4,900
σ = √4,900 = €70

Expected value is a theoretical average over a large number of policies. It does not mean the insurer will pay €10 for each phone: for a single policy, the cost is either zero or €500.

2. Pooling multiple policies

For n independent phones with the same risk, the number of thefts follows a binomial distribution B(n, 0.02). Expected thefts are np and the standard deviation is √(np(1 − p)). The standard deviation of average cost per phone is σ / √n.

E[N] = np   ;   σN = √(np(1 − p))
σX̄ = σ / √n = 70 / √n

4. What pooling cannot guarantee

At a premium of €11 per phone (€10 pure premium + €1 safety margin, expenses ignored), three or more thefts among 100 policies cost more than the premiums collected. For 10,000 policies, the threshold is 221 or more thefts.

  • Risks must be independent: a storm may affect many policies simultaneously.
  • Risks must be sufficiently similar to be grouped together.
  • There must be enough policies for averages to stabilise.
  • Claim probability must remain sufficiently stable.

5. Predictable does not mean profitable

The pure premium estimates expected claim cost. The commercial premium adds loadings (expenses, commissions, cost of capital and margin). Even a predictable average cost does not prevent an underwriting loss if pricing is too low, expenses too high or an extreme event occurs.

Important distinction: pooling reduces uncertainty around average cost; it does not automatically create a profit.

6. Worked exercise

An insurer covers 2,500 phones under the same assumptions. How many thefts does it expect? What is the standard deviation of average cost per phone?

50 expected thefts. Standard deviation of average cost: 70 / √2,500 = €1.40.

Key takeaways

  • For one policy: cost €0 or €500, expected value €10, standard deviation €70.
  • For n independent, identically distributed policies: standard deviation of average cost is σ / √n.
  • Pooling makes costs more predictable, but never guarantees a profit.
Fictional model: 2% annual theft probability, fixed €500 claim cost, at most one theft per phone, independent risks and expenses ignored.
