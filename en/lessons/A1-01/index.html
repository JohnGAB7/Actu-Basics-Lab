Actuarial path · A1.01

€1,000 today or in 30 years: is it really the same money?

How can we compare amounts paid at different dates? Let's build compound interest, then learn to move backwards through time using discounting.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish simple and compound interest.
  • Calculate a future value using the accumulation factor.
  • Calculate a present value by discounting a future payment.
  • Understand why the rate strongly affects present value.

1. Simple or compound interest

With simple interest, interest is always calculated on the original principal. At 5% per year on €1,000, we earn €50 each year. After 30 years, the total is €2,500.

FV = PV × (1 + i × n)

With compound interest, interest is added to the balance and then earns interest itself. Each year, we multiply the balance by 1.05.

FV = PV × (1 + i)n

3. Discounting: moving backwards in time

Discounting is the reverse of accumulation. To find today's value of a future payment, divide by the accumulation factor.

PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)n   ;   v = 1 ÷ (1 + i)

A €1,000 payment due in 30 years at a 5% rate has a present value of about €231.38. Invested at 5% for 30 years, €231.38 would grow back to about €1,000.

4. Why the rate matters

For a €1,000 payment due in 30 years, present value changes substantially with the discount rate. These rates are fictional and constant to illustrate the mechanism.

  • At 2%: about €552.07 today.
  • At 5%: about €231.38 today.
  • At 8%: about €99.38 today.
All else equal, a lower discount rate increases the present value of future payments. For an insurer, the effect depends on the applicable valuation framework.

5. Worked example

A fictional insurer must pay €10,000 in 10 years. At 4% per year, how much should it set aside today?

Answer: about €6,755.64. Calculation: 10,000 / 1.0410.

Key takeaways

  • Future value: PV × (1 + i)n.
  • Present value: FV ÷ (1 + i)n.
  • A lower rate produces a higher present value.
  • This model ignores tax, inflation and default risk.
Rates are fictional and constant. This educational example is not investment advice. The time value of money is not a model of inflation or purchasing power.
