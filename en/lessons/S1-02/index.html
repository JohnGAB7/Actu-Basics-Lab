Who is this number about? Population, unit, variable

“People in France sleep seven hours a night.” Before believing or comparing this figure, we need to know who was studied, which unit was observed and what was actually measured.

S1.0220–30 minSource script approved by JG · 8 November 2026

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish the target population, study population, sample and statistical unit.
  • Identify the observation unit, unit of analysis and variable in a study.
  • Explain why the same question can produce different results depending on the chosen unit.
  • Define a variable precisely and specify its measurement rule.
  • Read a statistic by checking its scope, period and denominator.

Prerequisite: S1.01 — understand what data, observations and variables are.

Guiding question. When a headline says “people in France sleep seven hours”, essential information is still missing: which people, observed how, over what period, and which average was calculated?

1. The three building blocks of a statistic

A statistical study links a set of units to one or more observed characteristics. To read a result correctly, start by identifying three elements.

Population

The set of units to which the research question refers. It must be defined using explicit criteria: territory, period, age, status or any other relevant condition.

Individual or statistical unit

The element on which an observation is made. Depending on the question, it may be a person, household, company, policy, claim, trip or day.

Variable

A characteristic recorded for each unit: sleep duration, monthly income, number of claims, policy type, and so on. A variable can take different values across units.

Population → statistical units → observed variables

Important: a statistical individual is not necessarily a person. In motor insurance, the unit may be a policy or an insured vehicle; in healthcare, a patient or care episode; in mobility, a trip or traveller. The choice depends on the question, not on a universal convention.

2. Define the population unambiguously

The word “population” is often used too quickly. Several distinct sets may be involved in a survey.

ConceptDefinitionSleep survey example
Target populationThe set about which we want to answer the research question.Adults living in metropolitan France on a specified date.
Study / covered populationThe set actually covered by the data collection system.Adults living in households covered by the survey design.
Sampling frameA list or system used to select units, when one exists.An address frame or register suited to the sampling design.
SampleUnits selected and contacted or actually observed.People randomly selected and interviewed.
Responding sampleUnits for which usable responses were obtained.People who actually completed the questionnaire.

These sets do not always coincide. A survey may target an entire population but cover only part of it, then obtain responses from an even smaller subset. Differences between these sets can create coverage or nonresponse problems.

Remember: a large sample is not automatically representative. Size alone cannot fix an incomplete sampling frame, biased selection or systematic nonresponse.

3. The chosen unit changes the question

Return to the fictional headline: “People in France sleep seven hours a night.” To interpret it, we need to specify at least:

  • Population: adults or all residents? Which territory? Which period?
  • Unit: one person, one observed night or one sleep episode?
  • Variable: total sleep duration, in hours or minutes.
  • Measurement: self-reported duration, a sleep diary or a sensor?
  • Summary: mean, median or another measure?

If ten nights are observed for each person, the rows in the data file may represent nights even though the research question concerns people. We must account for the fact that several observations come from the same individual. Ten nights from one person are not ten independent people.

The observation unit is what a measurement is recorded on. The unit of analysis is what the interpretation or conclusion concerns. They may differ, but that difference must be made explicit.

4. Same data, different average: a fictional village

Imagine three households. The incomes below are fictional monthly household totals.

HouseholdNumber of peopleMonthly household income
A1€2,000
B4€4,000
C2€3,000
Total7€9,000

Result 1 — average income per household

The statistical unit is the household. There are three units, with incomes of €2,000, €4,000 and €3,000.

(2,000 + 4,000 + 3,000) / 3 = €3,000 per household

Result 2 — total income divided by the number of residents

Divide the same total household income by the seven residents:

9,000 / 7 ≈ €1,285.71 per resident

The first indicator is average household income. The second relates aggregate household income to the number of people. It is not the average of observed individual incomes: we do not know how income is distributed within each household.

Correct interpretation: €3,000 per household and about €1,286 per resident answer two different questions. Do not present them as competing estimates of the same parameter.

5. A variable must be defined and measured

A variable is more than its name. For another person to understand or reproduce a study, we need an operational definition: the rule that turns an idea into a recorded value.

ConceptWhat must be specified?Possible values
Sleep durationSelf-reported or measured? Night-time sleep only? Which night or average?7.2 hours; 432 minutes
Monthly incomeGross or net? Individual or household? Which month? Which income components?€2,450 net; income category
Claims experienceReported, open or paid claims? Over which exposure period?0, 1, 2… claims
Policy typeWhich classification and reference date?Individual, group, another category

A measurement can be correct according to its rule while still being an imperfect representation of the concept the study is interested in. Self-reported sleep duration, for example, is not identical to duration measured by a device. Describe the method instead of assuming the two are interchangeable.

6. A checklist for reading any statistic

  1. Who? Define the target population and inclusion criteria.
  2. What unit? Identify the statistical unit and distinguish observation from analysis.
  3. Which characteristic? Name the variable, its categories or its unit.
  4. How? Describe the collection process, instrument and measurement rule.
  5. When and where? Specify the period and geographical or institutional scope.
  6. Which summary? Identify the numerator, denominator and calculated indicator.

7. Application exercises

EXERCISE 1 · IDENTIFY THE UNIT

An insurer studies amounts reimbursed for motor claims reported in 2025. Which unit best matches the variable “amount reimbursed per claim”?

Show the reasoned solution

Unit: the claim (or claim file, if one file corresponds exactly to one claim). The variable is the amount reimbursed for each claim. If the study concerned annual cost per policy, the unit would instead be the policy, and claims would need to be aggregated by policy.

EXERCISE 2 · POPULATION AND SAMPLE

A survey targets employees in a region. It is sent to employees listed in a professional address database; only 62% respond. Name two sets that must be distinguished and one methodological risk.

Show the reasoned solution

Distinguish the target population (employees in the region), people covered by the frame, people contacted and respondents. If nonrespondents have different working conditions or schedules, the results may be biased. The response rate alone does not reveal the direction or magnitude of the bias.

EXERCISE 3 · CHOOSE THE DENOMINATOR

In the fictional village, calculate average income per household and total income per resident. Why do these results measure different things?

Show the reasoned solution

Per household: 9,000 / 3 = €3,000. Per resident: 9,000 / 7 ≈ €1,285.71. The first uses households as units; the second relates the total to the number of residents. We do not know each person's individual income.

EXERCISE 4 · CRITIQUE A HEADLINE

“40% of residents use public transport.” Give at least four details needed before comparing this figure with another city.

Show the reasoned solution

Specify the population (residents, workers, adults…), territory and period, unit (person or trip), definition of “use” (at least once a week, main mode, commuting…), source and collection method. Also check the exact denominator and how missing responses were handled.

EXERCISE 5 · TRANSFER

A database contains 12,000 rows: 3,000 policies and possibly several claims per policy. Can we say it contains 12,000 insured people?

Show the reasoned solution

No. A row may represent a claim, policy or another unit. Check the data dictionary and identifier keys. The number of rows is not necessarily the number of policies, insured people or distinct individuals.

8. Summary and what comes next

  • The population defines who the research question refers to.
  • The statistical unit specifies what is observed.
  • The variable describes what is recorded for each unit.
  • The sample and sampling frame are not synonyms for the target population.
  • The choice of denominator and unit determines how a result should be interpreted.
  • A variable requires an operational definition and an explicit measurement method.
