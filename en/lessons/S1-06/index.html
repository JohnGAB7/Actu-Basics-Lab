Statistics & probability · S1.06

Pourquoi tire-t-on au sort dans les essais de médicaments ?

In a drug trial, neither the doctor nor the patient chooses who gets the treatment. Chance does. Why?

S1.06Source script approved by JG · 8 November 2026Lesson transcript and learning material

Lesson and narration

Each sequence shows the on-screen material and its corresponding narration. Exercises reveal the correction after you have had time to think.

Sequence 1 · Accroche
Why do drug trials assign treatment at random?
Read the narration

In a drug trial, neither the doctor nor the patient chooses who gets the treatment. Chance does. Why?

Sequence 2 · Chapitre
1. Observing
Read the narration

Let's start with another question. Does coffee help you live longer?

Sequence 3 · Étapes
An observational study (hypothetical)

  • Thousands of adults are followed for years

  • Each one chose whether to drink coffee

  • Coffee drinkers die at a slower rate

Read the narration

Imagine a study that follows thousands of adults for years. Each of them chose whether or not to drink coffee. At the end, coffee drinkers have died at a slower rate than the others.

Sequence 4 · Étapes
But the two groups already differ

  • Income, job

  • Smoking, diet

  • Health at the start

Read the narration

Can we conclude that coffee protects you? No. The two groups didn't form at random. They may differ in income, job, smoking, diet, or health at the start: someone who is ill sometimes drinks less coffee. Any of these differences could explain the gap.

Sequence 5 · Message
Observing shows a link, not a cause
Read the narration

An observational study can show a link. It can adjust for the differences it measured. But never for the ones it didn't measure. That's confounding, which we'll meet again in the lesson on correlation and causation.

Sequence 6 · Chapitre
2. Experimenting
Read the narration

In an experiment, we don't just observe: we intervene.

Sequence 7 · Étapes
A randomised trial

  • The researcher decides who gets the treatment

  • A control group gets a placebo or the usual treatment

  • Random assignment splits the participants

Read the narration

In a randomised trial, the researcher decides who gets the treatment. A control group gets a placebo, or the usual treatment, as a comparison. And a random draw splits the participants between the two groups.

Sequence 8 · Message
Chance favours no one
Read the narration

Why chance? Because nothing, not age, not smoking, not a factor nobody thought of, can systematically push some people towards the treatment. On average, across all possible draws, the two groups balance out. If a clear difference shows up afterwards, in a well-run trial, it can be put down to the treatment with more confidence, allowing for the margin of uncertainty.

Sequence 9 · Chapitre
3. Chance is no guarantee
Read the narration

Careful: on average doesn't mean every time.

Sequence 10 · Étapes
A simulated random draw

  • 20 volunteers, 7 of them smokers

  • Random draw: two groups of 10

  • Most often: 4 to 3

Read the narration

Let's simulate a small trial. Twenty volunteers, seven of them smokers. We split them at random into two groups of ten. Most often, the smokers are shared roughly evenly: four on one side, three on the other.

Sequence 11 · Chiffre
5.7% of draws give 6 to 1, or worse

Note: Hypergeometric distribution: P(6 or 7 smokers in one group) ≈ 5.7%. Checked calculation.
Read the narration

But in almost six percent of draws, about one in seventeen, you get six smokers to one, or even seven to none. Purely by chance.

Sequence 12 · Message
Hence the table of baseline characteristics
Read the narration

That's why trial results usually include a table comparing the groups at the start: age, sex, smoking, illnesses. It checks that chance hasn't created a troublesome imbalance. And the more participants a trial has, the rarer these chance gaps become.

Exercise · 13
Which studies are experiments?

8-second pause
Read the narration

Your turn. Four studies. A: comparing the health of children who play sport with those who don't. B: two hundred volunteers are drawn at random, half get a vaccine, half a placebo. C: retired people are asked about their past diet. D: a school draws lots to pick the classes that will test a new reading method. Which ones are experiments?


  • A and C: we observe choices already made

  • B and D: the researcher assigns at random




Answer: B and D: experiments
Read the narration

In A and C, we observe situations people chose or lived through: these are observational studies. In B and D, the split is decided by a random draw: these are experiments. If well designed and well analysed, they let you estimate an effect. Other methods exist, but random assignment is the most direct.

Sequence 15 · Étapes
Key takeaways

  • Observing: a link, not proof of a cause

  • Experimenting: random assignment balances on average

  • Always compare the groups at the start

Read the narration

Key takeaways. An observational study shows a link, not a cause. A randomised experiment balances the groups on average, which lets you conclude there's an effect. And even when drawn at random, the groups must be compared at the start. One last limit: not everything can be randomised. Nobody will make volunteers smoke for twenty years. Those questions need other methods, which we'll see later.
