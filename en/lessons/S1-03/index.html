Can you average postcodes? Types of variables

A postcode is written with digits. But is it a quantity? And is 20 °C twice as hot as 10 °C? It depends on the variable type and measurement scale.

S1.036–8 minScript approved by JG on 8 November 2026

Learning objectives

  • Identify the main concept and its definitions.
  • Interpret examples while keeping their assumptions in view.
  • Check your understanding with an exercise.

1. A number is not always a quantity

Let's average one postcode from Paris and one from Marseille:

(75,001 + 13,001) ÷ 2 = 44,001

The result 44,001 does not describe any “average” place. A postcode is a label written with digits, not a quantity.

2. Four types of variables

  • Nominal qualitative: a category with no order (colour, nationality, postcode).
  • Ordinal qualitative: a category with an order (satisfaction rating, hotel stars).
  • Discrete quantitative: a count (number of children).
  • Continuous quantitative: a measurement (height, duration, weight).

3. Measurement scales

  • Nominal: distinguish equal from different.
  • Ordinal: distinguish and rank.
  • Interval: compare meaningful differences.
  • Ratio: compare differences and ratios, thanks to an absolute zero.

4. Why 20 °C is not twice 10 °C

Twenty kilograms is twice ten kilograms: zero kilograms means no mass. In Celsius, zero degrees is a conventional reference point, not an absence of temperature.

10 °C = 283.15 K; 20 °C = 293.15 K
293.15 ÷ 283.15 ≈ 1.035

In kelvins, 20 °C is about 3.5% higher than 10 °C, not twice as much. Zero kelvin is absolute zero.

Averaging satisfaction scores from 1 to 5 assumes that the gaps between scores are comparable. If we make that assumption, we should say so.

5. Check your understanding

Exercise

Hotel stars from 1 to 5: what type of variable is this? Is a 4-star hotel twice as good as a 2-star hotel?
