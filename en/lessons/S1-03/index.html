Can you average postcodes?

A number is not necessarily a quantity. Learn to distinguish variable types and measurement scales so you can tell which comparisons, operations and statistics are meaningful.

S1.0310–15 min5 interactive exercisesPrerequisite: S1.02

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • classify a variable as categorical or numerical;
  • distinguish nominal, ordinal, discrete and continuous variables;
  • recognise the four measurement scales;
  • decide which statistical operations are justified;
  • spot common interpretation errors in data tables.
Building on S1.02. A population is studied through individuals and variables. S1.03 asks: once a variable has been identified, what do its values actually mean, and what can we legitimately do with them?

1. A number is not always a quantity

Consider two French postcodes: 75,001 for Paris and 13,001 for Marseille. Let's calculate their average:

(75,001 + 13,001) ÷ 2 = 44,001

The arithmetic is correct. Yet 44,001 does not represent a city halfway between Paris and Marseille, nor a meaningful “average” postcode. Why? Because these digits are labels. They identify an area; they do not measure an amount.

This is a fundamental statistical distinction: the format of a value does not reveal its nature. A phone number, customer ID or policy number may consist of digits without being a numerical variable.

2. The four main variable types

We first distinguish two broad families. A categorical variable describes a category or quality. A numerical variable expresses a countable or measurable quantity.

FamilyTypeDefinitionExamples
CategoricalNominalDistinct categories with no natural order.Postcode, colour, country, brand.
CategoricalOrdinalCategories that can be ranked.Low/medium/high satisfaction, risk level.
NumericalDiscreteValues obtained by counting; often integers.Number of claims, children or policies.
NumericalContinuousValues obtained by measurement on a continuum, subject to instrument precision.Duration, height, weight, measured amount.

2.1 Nominal: categories without a hierarchy

The categories are not naturally better, larger or smaller than one another. We can check whether two observations belong to the same category and count occurrences. The most frequent category is called the mode.

Example: the type of coverage in an insurance policy (assistance, liability, damage) is nominal. Replacing the labels with 1, 2 and 3 does not turn the variable into a quantity.

2.2 Ordinal: order, but not necessarily equal gaps

Categories can be ranked. We know that “high” is above “medium”, but we do not know whether the gap between low and medium is the same as the gap between medium and high.

Example: a satisfaction rating from 1 to 5. A rating of 4 indicates more satisfaction than 2, but this does not prove that the psychological distance is twice as large.

2.3 Discrete quantitative: counting

A discrete variable comes from counting. Between two consecutive integer claim counts, no other value is possible: a policy has 2 or 3 claims during the observed period, not 2.4 claims.

Note that a discrete variable is not necessarily small or limited. The number of transactions can take a very large number of integer values.

2.4 Continuous quantitative: measuring

A measurement can, in theory, take any value within an interval, depending on the precision used. A duration can be measured in hours, minutes, seconds or fractions of a second. A dataset may round it; the recording format does not necessarily change the nature of the underlying phenomenon.

Do not confuse the two. The number of decimal places displayed does not determine the variable type. A monetary amount rounded to the nearest euro remains a measurement; an identifier displayed with decimals would still be an identifier encoded incorrectly.

3. The four measurement scales

Variable type tells us what is being described. The measurement scale tells us which comparisons are justified by the meaning of the values. Four scales are commonly distinguished.

ScaleWhat it allowsExampleCommon summaries
NominalSame / different; category membership.Region, colour, postcode.Counts, frequencies, proportions, mode.
OrdinalSame / different; greater / lower ranking.Ranking, satisfaction, risk level.Counts, frequencies, median, quantiles, ranks.
IntervalDifferences are meaningful; zero is conventional.Temperature in °C, calendar dates.Differences, mean, standard deviation, correlation depending on context.
RatioDifferences and ratios; zero means none of the quantity.Duration, mass, distance, amount, claim count.Arithmetic and common statistical operations, subject to context.

3.1 Nominal scale: distinguish and count

We can calculate the proportion of policies by coverage type, but the average of numeric codes assigned to coverage categories is meaningless. If “assistance” is recoded from 1 to 7 and “damage” from 3 to 9, the categories have not changed; only their numeric labels have.

3.2 Ordinal scale: rank without measuring the gap

We can say that one observation is ranked above another. The median and quantiles are often appropriate because they rely on order. By contrast, the mean and standard deviation require an additional interpretation of the distances between categories.

3.3 Interval scale: differences are meaningful

On the Celsius scale, the difference between 10 °C and 20 °C is 10 degrees, just as the difference between 20 °C and 30 °C is. But 0 °C does not mean an absence of temperature. We can interpret differences, not ratios of values.

3.4 Ratio scale: zero means absence

A duration of 20 minutes is twice a duration of 10 minutes. Zero minutes means no elapsed duration. The same reasoning applies to mass, distance or cost, provided the quantity and unit are properly defined.

4. Why 20 °C is not twice 10 °C

Zero Celsius is a conventional reference point. To compare ratios of thermodynamic temperature, we use the Kelvin scale, whose zero corresponds to absolute zero.

10 °C = 283.15 K
20 °C = 293.15 K
293.15 ÷ 283.15 ≈ 1.035

On the Kelvin scale, the absolute temperature corresponding to 20 °C is about 1.035 times the temperature corresponding to 10 °C, or about 3.5% higher. It is not twice as high. This does not mean that perceived warmth increases by 3.5%: human sensation is a different concept, also affected by humidity, wind and context.

5. Variable type and measurement scale: two different questions

The four variable types and the four measurement scales are related, but they should not be mechanically confused. A qualitative ordinal variable has an ordinal scale. A measured quantitative variable may use an interval or ratio scale. Numeric codes assigned to a categorical variable do not make it quantitative.

Real cases depend on context: age recorded in whole years is often treated as discrete in a dataset, even though elapsed time is continuous. A five-level satisfaction score is ordinal, even if some statistical models treat it as approximately numerical under explicit assumptions.

6. Can we calculate an average?

The arithmetic mean is not a universal operation. It is generally meaningful for numerical quantities on interval or ratio scales. For an ordinal variable, calculating a mean assumes that the gaps between categories are sufficiently comparable for the chosen analysis.

  • Postcode: no meaningful average; use counts or proportions by area.
  • Satisfaction 1–5: show the distribution and median; a mean may be added if its assumption is explained.
  • Claim count: average claims per policy is interpretable if the scope and exposure are defined.
  • Claim cost: a mean is possible, but beware of skewness and extreme claims.
Professional reflex. Before calculating a mean, ask: what is the unit? What does zero mean? Are the gaps comparable? Are observations comparable, and is the denominator correctly defined?

7. Applications in statistics and actuarial work

An insurance database may contain all of the following:

  • a postcode (nominal);
  • a low, medium or high risk level (ordinal);
  • a claim count (discrete quantitative);
  • a claim cost in euros (quantitative, ratio scale);
  • a reporting date (interval scale for elapsed time, with calendar-related caveats).

This classification guides charts, summaries, tests and models. A nominal variable may be represented by frequencies or encoded as indicator variables in a model. An ordinal variable calls for preserving its order. A quantitative variable can be summarised using measures of location and spread suited to its distribution.

8. Key takeaways

  • A number can be an identifier, a rank or a quantity.
  • Nominal categorical: categories without order.
  • Ordinal categorical: ordered categories, with no guaranteed equal gaps.
  • Discrete quantitative: count; continuous quantitative: measure.
  • Nominal: distinguish; ordinal: rank; interval: compare differences; ratio: compare ratios too.
  • “Twice as much” requires a meaningful zero and a ratio scale.
