Introduction to actuarial science · A0.01

Actuaries: the profession and a first calculation.

How can an uncertain risk be turned into a numerical estimate? Let's begin with the role of actuaries, the basics of an insurance contract and a pure premium calculation.

Introduction5–8 minutesInsurance · Risk · Probability

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • explain the role of actuaries in measuring risk;
  • identify the essential components of an insurance contract;
  • calculate a pure premium from claim frequency and average claim cost.

1. The role of actuaries

Actuaries use mathematics, probability and statistics to analyse uncertain risks and inform financial and insurance decisions. Their work involves quantifying the possible consequences of future events using data, models and assumptions.

They do not predict the future with certainty. They build estimates consistent with the available information and make the uncertainty behind those estimates explicit.

2. Components of an insurance contract

An insurance contract specifies covered events, exclusions, the premium paid by the policyholder and benefits paid when a claim occurs. It may also include a deductible: the part of a loss that remains the policyholder's responsibility.

For an insurer, the challenge is to estimate the future cost of claims across a portfolio of policies. This is where data and probability become useful.

3. Calculating a pure premium

The pure premium is the expected average claim cost per policy, before expenses, commissions and any risk margin.

Pure premium = expected frequency × average claim cost

Consider a fictional portfolio of 1,000 phones. Each phone has a 2% annual probability of theft. Each theft costs exactly €500.

Step 1 — Expected number of thefts

Apply the annual theft probability to the number of phones:

1,000 × 2% = 20 expected thefts

Step 2 — Expected total cost

Multiply the expected number of thefts by the cost of one theft:

20 × €500 = €10,000

Step 3 — Pure premium per phone

Allocate the expected total cost across the 1,000 insured phones:

€10,000 ÷ 1,000 = €10
Key point. In this example, the pure premium is €10 per phone per year. This is not necessarily the commercial price paid by the customer: expenses, commissions and margins are not included.

4. Assumptions

The result relies on the following simplifying assumptions:

  • the portfolio contains 1,000 phones;
  • the annual theft probability is 2% per phone;
  • at most one theft can occur per phone during the year;
  • each theft costs exactly €500.

In a real portfolio, these assumptions would need to be examined: observed frequency, changes in risk, differences between policyholders and uncertainty in claim costs.
