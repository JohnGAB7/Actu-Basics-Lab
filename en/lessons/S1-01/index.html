Statistics & probability · Level 1 · S1.01

How many people live in Paris? What counts as data?

2.1 million, 11 million or 13.3 million? The three figures can all be correct. The answer depends on what we mean by “Paris”, what we count, and how we measure it.

S1.0125–35 minSource script approved by JG · 8 November 2026

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish a statistical question from a question with one fixed answer.
  • Define a statistical unit, target population, sample, variable, value and observation.
  • Explain why a number is inseparable from its operational definition, perimeter, date and method.
  • Represent a dataset as an observations-by-variables matrix and recognise tidy data.
  • Identify measurement error, missing values, selection bias and limits to comparability.

Prerequisites: No formal prerequisite. Comfort with reading whole numbers and simple tables is enough.

A statistic is not just a number. It is an answer produced by a process: a question is asked, a population and a unit are defined, a characteristic is observed, a method is applied, and a result is recorded.

1. From a question to statistical variability

“How tall is the Eiffel Tower?” normally asks for one value under a specified measurement convention. “How long do commuters take to reach work?” asks for many values. They differ across people, journeys and days. A statistical question anticipates this variability and seeks to describe or explain it.

Variability is not noise to be eliminated by definition. It is often the very phenomenon statistics is designed to study.

2. The vocabulary of a statistical study

These terms are related, but they are not interchangeable. Define them before collecting or analysing anything.

TermMeaningParis example
Statistical unitThe entity on which information is collected.A resident, a household, a municipality, depending on the study.
Target populationAll units about which the study aims to make statements.All usual residents within a precisely defined geographic boundary.
SampleThe subset of units actually observed.The people selected and responding to a travel survey.
VariableA characteristic recorded for each unit.Age, usual residence, commute duration.
ValueThe recorded value of a variable for one unit.34 years; 42 minutes; “Paris”.
Observation / recordThe collection of values associated with one unit.One row describing one respondent.
Measurement / operational definitionThe rule and procedure used to turn a concept into a recorded value.Door-to-door commute time on a usual working day, in minutes.

A concept such as “commute time” is not self-measuring. Does it include walking to the station? Waiting? A school drop-off? Is it the last trip or a typical trip? A measurement protocol makes the concept operational. Different protocols can produce different, individually valid answers.

3. The Paris example: three valid perimeters

The validated source script uses Insee’s 2023 population figures, published in its territory comparator. The same word — Paris — can refer to different geographic objects:

Geographic definitionPopulation (2023)What it includes
Paris municipality2,103,778The administrative municipality.
Paris urban unit11,013,774The continuously built-up urban area.
Paris functional / attraction area13,320,752The wider commuting area, including municipalities linked to the employment core.

The attraction-area definition used in the source script includes surrounding municipalities where at least 15% of employed residents work in the pole. The ratio 13,320,752 ÷ 2,103,778 is approximately 6.33. This is not a discrepancy in arithmetic: the numerator and denominator count people in different geographic populations.

Comparability rule: before comparing two population figures, align the geographic boundary, reference date, population concept, and statistical source. A shared label does not guarantee a shared definition.

Before comparing a statistic, ask: what exactly was counted, where, when, and how?

4. A dataset is a structured collection of observations

Suppose a small, entirely synthetic travel survey records three people. Rows represent statistical units; columns represent variables. The entry xᵢⱼ is the value of variable j for unit i.

Unit iAge X₁ (years)Commute X₂ (minutes)Mode X₃
12935Train
24152Car
33428Bike

In general, a rectangular dataset with n units and p variables can be represented by X = (xᵢⱼ), where i = 1,…,n and j = 1,…,p. This notation is a compact description of the data structure, not a claim that the values are error-free or independent.

X = [xᵢⱼ]ₙₓₚ

A tidy-data convention is useful: each variable has its own column, each observation its own row, and each observational unit its own table. Real projects may require several linked tables — for example, one table for people and another for trips — rather than forcing everything into one giant sheet.

5. Data quality: what can go wrong?

Coverage and selection

Are all relevant units represented? A convenience sample of people at one station may miss other commuters.

Nonresponse

Do selected people who do not respond differ from respondents? Weighting may help, but cannot automatically remove all bias.

Measurement

Does the question or instrument capture the intended concept? Recall, rounding and ambiguous wording can matter.

Recording and coding

Were units, dates, categories and decimal separators recorded consistently?

Missingness

Is a value absent, not applicable, refused, unknown, or not collected? These states should not be silently treated as zero.

Time and version

Which reference period, extraction date, geographic version and data revision are being used?

A large dataset is not automatically a good dataset. A million systematically mismeasured records can be less useful than a carefully designed smaller sample. Quality must be assessed relative to the intended use.

6. A practical workflow

  1. State the question and the decision the analysis should inform.
  2. Define the target population, statistical unit, time period and geographic scope.
  3. Specify each variable and its operational definition, unit and allowed values.
  4. Document the sampling or collection process and the source version.
  5. Inspect duplicates, impossible values, missingness, coding and unit consistency.
  6. Only then summarise, compare or model the observations.

7. Guided exercises

EXERCISE 1

A weather station records the temperature at 14:00 today. Is this automatically a statistical question?

Show the correction

Not necessarily. If the question is the single temperature at one station and one instant, it seeks one value. A question about temperatures across 50 stations or across 30 days anticipates variability.

EXERCISE 2

A survey asks 500 randomly selected workers for their usual one-way commute time. Identify the unit, sample and variable.

Show the correction

Unit: an individual worker. Sample: the 500 selected workers (more precisely, the respondents among them if some do not answer). Variable: usual one-way commute duration, with a stated unit and definition.

EXERCISE 3

Why can 2.1 million and 13.3 million both describe Paris?

Show the correction

They refer to different geographic populations: the municipality and the wider attraction area. The values should not be compared as if the perimeter were identical.

EXERCISE 4

A spreadsheet uses 0 for both “no answer” and “no commute”. What is the problem?

Show the correction

It conflates missing information with a real value or a not-applicable state. Preserve distinct codes or explicit missing-value labels and document them.

8. Mini-lab: inspect a dataset

The following tiny dataset is synthetic and exists only for practice. Run the same basic inspection in R or Python; the goal is to understand the shape and meaning of the data before computing summaries.

R

d <- data.frame(
  id = 1:5,
  age = c(29, 41, 34, NA, 52),
  commute_min = c(35, 52, 28, 40, NA),
  mode = c("train", "car", "bike", "bus", "train")
)
str(d)
summary(d)
colSums(is.na(d))

Python

import pandas as pd

d = pd.DataFrame({
    "id": [1, 2, 3, 4, 5],
    "age": [29, 41, 34, None, 52],
    "commute_min": [35, 52, 28, 40, None],
    "mode": ["train", "car", "bike", "bus", "train"]
})
print(d.info())
print(d.describe(include="all"))
print(d.isna().sum())
Questions to investigate
  • How many rows and variables are present?
  • Which variables are numeric and which are categorical?
  • How many missing values occur in each column?
  • Why should the missing age and commute time not be replaced by zero without justification?

Key takeaways

  • A statistical question anticipates variability across observations.
  • Every variable needs a clear unit, definition, reference period and measurement procedure.
  • The unit, population and sample are distinct concepts.
  • A dataset is structured; its rows and columns have a statistical meaning.
  • Data quality and provenance must be checked before analysis.
